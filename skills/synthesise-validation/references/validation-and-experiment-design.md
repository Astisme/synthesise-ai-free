# Validation and experiment design

## 1. Build a hypothesis architecture

Create separately testable hypotheses for customer/segment, problem, urgency, solution, differentiation, acquisition, delivery feasibility, buying behaviour and willingness to pay. Give each a stable ID, owner, statement, scope, evidence, confidence, consequence if false and status. Avoid compound hypotheses that cannot be falsified by one experiment.

## 2. Prioritize uncertainty

Assess each assumption on impact if false, uncertainty, cost of being wrong, test cost/time and dependency reach. Use a transparent ordinal ranking or a documented calculation; do not imply statistical precision. Test assumptions that could invalidate the opportunity before optimizing low-risk details.

## 3. Choose the experiment to match the claim

- Problem context: recent-event interviews, observation or diary study.
- Frequency/prevalence: appropriately sampled survey or longitudinal log.
- Concept comprehension: concept test with neutral prompts.
- Usability: task-based prototype test with observed completion and friction.
- Acquisition: qualified landing-page or channel test with a defined denominator.
- Delivery feasibility: concierge or limited pilot with operational measures.
- Payment: transparent paid pilot, deposit or pre-sale with clear terms.
- Learning effectiveness: pilot assessment of learner performance, not satisfaction alone.

State what each method can and cannot establish. Interviews can reveal mechanisms and language, not population prevalence. Clicks can indicate attention, not purchase intent. A paid commitment is stronger payment evidence, but still scoped to the tested offer, audience and conditions.

## 4. Design the protocol before execution

Record hypothesis ID, decision to inform, participant criteria, exclusions, recruitment source, sample rationale, script/tasks, method, duration, cost, instrumentation, consent/privacy approach, success/failure thresholds, stop rule and analysis plan. Define thresholds before observing results. If a threshold changes, preserve the original and explain the deviation.

## 5. Control sampling and measurement bias

Document convenience/snowball sampling, self-selection, non-response, survivorship, leading questions, social desirability, channel effects, duplicate participants and instrumentation failures. Track the denominator and exclusions. Do not generalize beyond the recruited population. Preserve raw notes or aggregates with consent and data minimization.

## 6. Analyze results

Separate raw observations, coded themes, interpretation and recommendation. Report counts with denominators, dates, sample characteristics and missing data. Include negative cases and contradictory evidence. Distinguish protocol completion from a successful result. Update confidence only with a reasoned account of evidence strength and limitations.

## 7. Make an explicit decision

Use proceed, revise, retest or stop. Tie the decision to the predeclared rule; explain exceptions. A proceed decision authorizes only the next bounded investment, not an unqualified claim that the market is validated. A stop decision preserves learning and states which premise failed. Record dissent and unresolved uncertainty.

## 8. Reusable experiment record

Each experiment record includes ID, hypothesis IDs, version, status (planned/running/completed/invalidated), dates, protocol, sample/recruitment, thresholds, results, deviations, limitations, evidence IDs, decision and next test. Never overwrite an earlier protocol or silently upgrade an interest signal into purchase evidence.

## 9. Quality review

Check falsifiability, method-fit, predeclared thresholds, recruitment transparency, denominator integrity, provenance, contradictory evidence and bounded conclusions. If the experiment did not run, label its results as not collected.
